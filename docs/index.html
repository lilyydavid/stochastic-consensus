A Method for AI-Assisted Research

What’s worth teaching a child when AI can answer everything?

For a lot of parents, raising a child now feels like an arms race. Ballet on Saturday, German tutoring on Sunday, maths after school — lessons booked in the hope they’ll buy a child something later on: a place, a network, an edge.

But nobody can say what “later on” looks like anymore. Which raises an awkward question. If you can’t predict the future, what do you actually invest in? What holds its value no matter how things turn out?

What the research pointed to

So I asked AI which skills had survived every upheaval before this one — the invention of fire, the printing press, the industrial revolution, the internet. The same three kept coming back.

Metacognition thinking about your own thinking
Adaptive Judgment deciding well without a map
Complex Communication making others truly understand

Coding camps and tech-literacy courses didn’t make the cut. The pattern across decades of labour data is boringly consistent: machines take the repeatable work, and the value of these three goes up, not down.

Which is a tidy answer.

Putting the answer to the test

But was it really that simple? The only way to know was to test it — and the honest way to do that is to sit down with real parents and real children and ask. That’s the proper next step.

Before booking all those conversations, though, I wanted a faster way to find the weak spots. Ever tried stochastic consensus? I decided to give it a spin: I ran each idea past a crowd of invented parents and kids — a single parent with no time, an expat family always on the move, a parent who can’t watch their kid struggle — and let them argue with it.

Everything below is synthetic. The personas, quotes, and observations were generated by AI agents as proxies for user research — hypotheses about what parents and children might say, not evidence of what real ones have said. What follows is a curated subset of foundational practices, not the full list.

Four practices, stress-tested

1

Managing Boredom

Ages 3–6
The AI answer

Give them a daily stretch of nothing-to-do, no screens, and let creativity fill the gap.

The simulation pushes back

“When he follows me from room to room whining that he’s bored, it doesn’t feel like a creative breakthrough — it feels like I’m neglecting him after a grueling day at work. The guilt makes it almost impossible not to hand him a screen just to make it stop.”

Parent

“I don’t know what to do.” “I feel like I am being punished.”

Child
Where it breaks

Boredom only turns into invention when a child knows a place inside out. Kids who move a lot — or have no community nearby — get anxiety instead. And a bored child trailing you around the house is usually asking for closeness, not stimulation.

2

Low-Stakes Sports

Ages 6–9
The AI answer

Put the child in a sport they’re not naturally good at, often enough that they lose for real and keep showing up.

The simulation pushes back

“The hard part was never cost or scheduling — it was my own restraint. I started bringing a book to every session so I physically couldn’t coach or hover from the sideline.”

Parent

“I don’t know if I’m getting better or just getting used to feeling bad.” “Watching my mum clapping too hard from the bench somehow makes failing feel worse.”

Child
Where it breaks

The hard part isn’t the cost — it’s the sideline. Kids say being watched is what makes it worse, not the losing. And failure only builds resilience if they can see themselves improving; without that, they just get used to being stuck.

3

Structured Family Arguments

Ages 6–9
The AI answer

A weekly, screen-free argument where the adult disagrees and won’t fold, so the child has to make and defend a case.

The simulation pushes back

“This is incredibly difficult because I hate conflict at home. The second my child gets upset or says something completely wrong, my instinct is to step in, correct them, and shut the argument down.”

Parent

“My face goes hot, I worry that Dad will be cross with me afterward, and I just want to say ‘you’re right’ just to make it stop.”

Child
Where it breaks

Weekly is a fantasy that just breeds guilt. And the moment a child gets upset or says something wrong, most adults can’t help softening, correcting, or shutting it down.

4

Continuous Discipline through Music

Ages 9–12
The AI answer

Have them practise an instrument until they can play it for someone without flinching — the discipline of getting ready for a moment that matters.

The simulation pushes back

“By the time I’ve played chauffeur, calendar manager, and practice cop to get them to sit down with the instrument, I have zero emotional bandwidth left to just sit back, listen, and appreciate what they are playing.”

Parent

“I enjoy playing the guitar alone with the door closed.” “I don’t know if I would miss the violin, or if I would just miss being the girl who is known for being good at the violin.”

Child
Where it breaks

The parent becomes scheduler, teacher, enforcer, driver and only audience all at once — with no patience left to actually enjoy the playing. And the quiet practice that is the real discipline gets buried under all that managing.

What I took away

Take a problem you can’t predict the answer to. Use AI to match it against the patterns — decades of research compressed into a clean recommendation. Then don’t just take its word for it.

Challenge it with something like stochastic consensus — a panel of clashing, invented perspectives whose only job is to find where the neat answer falls apart. You don’t come out with a solution. You come out with a sharper map of the gaps, and a much better sense of what to actually go and test.
